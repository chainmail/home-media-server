## What the dog likely experienced

A dog that was physically close to a suicide would have been exposed to:

- **Sensory trauma**: the smell of blood, the visual of a collapsed/dead body, sounds (the act itself, the carer's distress, paramedics)
- **A sudden shift in the carer's emotional state**: the dog's primary attachment figure went from calm to terrified/distressed in an instant. For a dog, the carer's fear is itself a threat signal — the dog essentially experienced *two* trauma sources simultaneously: the event and the carer's panic.
- **Loss of environmental safety**: the space where this happened is now "unsafe" in the dog's nervous system, even though nothing physically threatened them.

## Phased approach

The key insight from recent trauma-informed canine care literature (e.g., TAO Animal Center, 2025) is that **standard desensitization protocols can fail or backfire if applied too early** to a dog in acute trauma. The nervous system needs to be regulated *before* it can process and re-associate. The work happens in phases:

### Phase 1: Acute stabilization (first 1–3 weeks)

This is **not** a training phase. The goal is to let the dog's nervous system come down from threat mode.

- **No demands, no commands, no corrections.** The dog should not be asked to perform anything. If they refuse to come when called, let them. If they hide, don't pull them out.
- **Freedom of movement and choice.** The dog should be able to approach or retreat from any area, person, or object without being directed. This restores *agency* — the core thing the traumatic event removed.
- **Food freely available**, not in a puzzle or at a set spot. No pressure to eat, but food should be easy to access.
- **Minimize the carer's visible distress around the dog.** This is critical. The dog is reading the carer's state. The carer does not need to perform "calm" — they need to *not be in acute crisis* in the dog's presence. If the carer is in a state of shock, panic, or sobbing, the dog should be given a separate safe space (a quiet room with a human who is not in that state) until the carer has regulated.
- **A safe space**: a specific room, crate (if the dog is crate-comfortable), or elevated area where the dog can be undisturbed. No visitors, no children, no dogs in this space.
- **Reduce environmental complexity**: fewer people in the home, no new routines, no changes to the living space if possible.
- **Monitor but don't intervene**: note what the dog does (where they sleep, what they avoid, appetite, elimination) without trying to "fix" it. This log is valuable for the behaviorist.

### Phase 2: Nervous system regulation (weeks 3–8, roughly)

Once the dog is eating, sleeping, and no longer in constant fight/flight/freeze, you can begin gently rebuilding their sense of safety.

- **Reintroduce routine slowly**: set feeding times, short calm walks (not to the location of the event), gentle play.
- **Sniff walks**: let the dog lead and sniff. This is a naturally calming activity that engages the parasympathetic nervous system.
- **Mental enrichment at low intensity**: lick mats, snuffle mats, simple food puzzles. Nothing that requires the dog to "perform" — just self-directed foraging.
- **Physical exercise**: moderate, not intense. A tired dog is a calmer dog, but over-exercising a traumatized dog can increase cortisol.
- **Calm, predictable human energy**: the carer (or another household member) should be the primary source of safety. Steady, slow, unhurried. No sudden movements, no loud voices.
- **Dog appeasing pheromones** (Adaptil collar or diffuser) can be a useful adjunct at this stage.

### Phase 3: Targeted desensitization (only when the dog is stable)

This is where a **veterinary behaviorist or certified applied animal behaviorist (CAAB)** should be guiding the process. The triggers to address will likely include:

| Likely trigger | Desensitization approach |
|---|---|
| The location/room where the event occurred | Start with the dog at a distance (e.g., in another room), pair with high-value treats. Gradually decrease distance over *weeks*, only when the dog is fully relaxed at each step. Never force entry. |
| The sight/smell of blood | Not practical to desensitize directly; the location work covers this. If the dog is triggered by the *smell*, the room needs to be thoroughly cleaned before any re-exposure. |
| Emergency vehicle sounds (sirens) | Play recordings at very low volume, pair with treats, increase volume over many sessions. |
| Unfamiliar people (paramedics, police, visitors) | Start with a calm, still person at a distance. The person does nothing — no talking, no reaching, no eye contact. Treats are dropped, not handed. Gradually decrease distance over sessions. |
| The carer's own distress (if the dog associates the carer's emotional state with danger) | This is harder. The carer needs to model calm. If the carer is still in acute trauma, a second household member or a professional should take over as the dog's "safe person" temporarily. |

**Critical rule**: the stimulus must stay *below* the dog's reaction threshold at every step. If the dog shows any sign of stress (panting, lip-licking, turning away, pinned ears, lifting a paw), the intensity is too high — back off. Pushing past threshold *sensitizes* rather than desensitizes.

## Medication

Given the severity of the exposure (physical proximity to a death), **a veterinary behaviorist consultation is strongly recommended within the first 1–2 weeks**, not after symptoms have been "waited out." The behaviorist will assess whether the dog needs:

- **Anxiolytics** (e.g., trazodone, gabapentin) for acute episodes
- **SSRI/SNRI** (e.g., fluoxetine, clomipramine) if the baseline anxiety is elevated enough to prevent behavioral work from being effective
- **Beta-blockers** (e.g., propranolol) for situational physiological arousal

Medication is not a crutch — it lowers the noise floor so the dog's brain can actually process and re-associate. It's typically tapered once progress is made.

## The carer's own trauma

This is not a side note — it's a central factor. The carer was physically present at a suicide. They are almost certainly in acute trauma themselves. A dog in a household where the primary attachment figure is in a state of shock, hypervigilance, or emotional collapse will **not** be able to regulate, because the dog is using the carer as their safety reference.

- The carer should seek **trauma-informed psychological support** (not just grief counselling — this is trauma, and the two require different approaches).
- If the carer is not in a state to be the dog's safe person, **another household member or a professional (a force-free trainer, a pet sitter) should take over as the dog's primary attachment figure** for the acute phase.

## When to escalate

Contact a veterinary behaviorist **urgently** (not "in a few weeks") if:
- The dog is in a persistent freeze/shutdown state (won't eat, won't move, unresponsive) for more than 48–72 hours
- There is any aggression, even a single incident
- The dog is self-injuring (excessive licking, biting themselves)
- Symptoms are clearly worsening rather than stabilizing after the first 2 weeks

## Summary

This is a **trauma case, not a grief case**. The dog needs a phased, trauma-informed approach: **safety and agency first, regulation second, desensitization third.** The carer's own healing is part of the dog's treatment. A veterinary behaviorist should be involved early, not as a last resort.
